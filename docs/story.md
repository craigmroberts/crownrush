# Story bible — draft four

Draft three was a cosmology: nine sisters, a woman buried alive for eight hundred years, a
two-hundred-year-old gaoler, a bloodline fled south through eight generations. It was internally
sound and nobody could hold it, including the person who owns the game. That is the only test a
story like this has to pass, and it failed it.

**This draft is built the other way round.** It starts from what the game already does, and it stops
explaining as soon as the mechanics are covered.

Three rules, unchanged from draft three because they were right:

1. **Never stop the game.** Every line is one or two sentences, barked over play.
2. **The story explains what the player is already doing** — the walls, the nights, the Queen.
3. **A twist must recontextualise something the player has already done**, not something they were
   told.

---

## The whole of it, in one paragraph

Something under the mountains in the north will not let the dead stay dead. Somebody has to hold it
shut, and whoever has been holding it is failing. The dead walk south every night — your own dead,
eight hundred years of them — and the men who come with them are not raiders. They are collectors,
and they came for your wife, because she is the only person left who can take the weight.

That is the entire cosmology. Everything below is that sentence pointed at things the game already
ships.

---

## The opening: you had a kingdom this morning

**The player starts inside the finished village.** Walls up, towers crewed, villagers working, the
Keep at its full height, Wren walking a step behind him. Everything he is about to spend half an hour
rebuilding, standing and working, for one quiet minute.

Then it falls. Not a raid — the fall. It is overwhelming and it is meant to be lost.

**And they walk past him.** They do not attack the King. He can stand in front of them and they go
around. They are not here for him, and the player watches that happen before anybody has a word for
it.

They take her north. He wakes at dawn on the rubble, which is where the game as built actually
begins.

This does three jobs at once and that is why it is the opening:

- It explains the empty plot. A king with no village needs a reason, and "it was destroyed last
  night" is the whole of it.
- It is a tutorial that costs nothing. The player has *seen* a finished village work, so every mat he
  later stands on is a thing he remembers having. No tooltip teaches that.
- It plants the twist in behaviour, in minute two. When Bramble says *"they're not raiders, love.
  Raiders want something"* twenty nights later, it lands on something he lived through.

> **CORVYN:** Rebuild it. Every time. That's the job.

---

## Who Wren is — and no, not a witch

She is not a practising anything. She has never cast, brewed or summoned, and she would be the first
to say so.

**She is an inheritance.** The duty of holding the thing in the north shut passes down a bloodline,
the current holder is dying, and Wren is the last person alive who can take it. She does not know
this. Nobody has told her. She has started dreaming of a hill with a door in it and has not mentioned
it to anyone, because saying it out loud would make it a thing.

The distinction matters for how she plays. A witch is a character with abilities, and the game would
have to give her some. An heir is a character with a **price**, and the game already charges it: she
is what the raids come for, she is why the Keep exists, and losing her twice ends the run.

The word *witch* belongs to Bramble, who uses it as a job title and means it.

> **NELL:** It's a hill with a door in it. And someone inside knows my name. She's very polite about it.

**The ending is her decision, not yours.** Whether she spends the rest of her life under a mountain
holding a hand over somebody's mouth is the question the whole game is walking towards, and the King
does not get a vote.

## The rules of the dead — and what they already are in the game

Every row is something the code already does. The story only says why. This table is the most
valuable thing in this document and it is unchanged from draft three, because it was the part that
was working.

| Already in the game | Now it means |
| --- | --- |
| Raids only come at night | **The taken cannot hold their shape in daylight.** Dawn is not a reward, it is a curfew that happens to suit you |
| Blood moon every fifth night | The nights the hold slips worst. More come, and something old comes with them |
| Enemy ranks by colour | **How long they have been dead.** Tan: a few years, still mostly people. Red: a generation. Purple: centuries. Black and gold: wars nobody alive remembers |
| Raiders drop coins | **They carry what they were buried with.** You are collecting grave goods out of your own churchyards. Nobody feels good about it and nobody stops |
| Sappers ignore your army | The ones so far gone there is nothing left but the going. *"That one isn't coming for us. He's coming for the wall."* |
| Enemy archers | **Your own longbowmen.** Your grandfathers, in your colours, shooting at your walls |
| Shieldbearers | Still standing behind the shields they died behind. Hit them from the side and they do not know how to turn |
| **Thieves** | **See below. This is the big one** |

### The Rooks

The thieves are collectors too, and **they do not steal your gold.**

A Rook runs onto the field, picks up **one of your fallen soldiers**, throws him over its shoulder and
runs for the map edge.

- Cut it down and you get your man back. He is buried, and that is the end of it.
- Let it reach the edge and **that soldier is in the next raid.** In your colours. With the training
  upgrades you paid for.

It costs you a body and gives them one, it makes every death on your walls worth running into the
open for, and it is the best available use of a mechanic that already ships. The current thief steals
coins. This one steals Dain, who joined on night four.

## The cast

Four people. Draft three had eight and that was three too many.

### King Corvyn — the player
Young, unglamorous, stubborn, and the first king in four generations to do any of the work himself.
Married a blacksmith's daughter and never explained himself about it. His answer to the supernatural
is to fetch a hammer and ask what it is made of.

### Wren — the second lead, and the prize
Not highborn. Broad hands, no patience for court, funny in a way that alarms people. The last of a
line she has never heard of. Dreaming of a mountain since the night they took her.

### The Rust — world one's enemy, and the twist
A man in rusted armour who has not taken his helm off in twenty years.

He is **Ser Aldric Vane**, the kingdom's greatest knight, who rode north twenty years ago to end the
raids for good and never came back. There is a statue of him in the square and the King can see it
from the Keep steps. He did not die. He was taken and given what they give, and for twenty years he
has been the thing he rode out to destroy. There are people alive who would know the face.

**He has been trying to lose for eleven years.** He is very good at his job and cannot help it.

At 20% health he stops fighting and opens his arms. Not a phase change — he simply stops.

> **THE RUST:** Come on then. Come on. Don't make me ask twice. I've been asking for eleven years.

Then the helm comes off.

> **ILKA:** *(quietly)* Oh. They'll have to take the statue down.
> **CORVYN:** They will not.

### Bramble — the hedge-witch
The village wise woman. Chickens, poultices, a filthy sense of humour, seventy-odd. The first person
in the game to say *witch* and mean it as a job title. She knows more than she says and has known it
for forty years.

> **BRAMBLE:** Oh, they're not raiders, love. Raiders want something. Look at their *feet*.

## Act structure — world one

The Keep level is the story clock, because it is already the difficulty clock and the HUD already
shows it as `Lv.`

| Lv. | Mechanical beat (exists) | Story beat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| — | The fall, and the rescue | They walk past you to get to her |
| 1 | Keep raised | The first night. Corvyn asks why they only come after dark |
| 2 | Expansion | Ser Bracken rides in, unasked, and is immediately useful |
| 3 | Barracks · sappers | *"That one isn't coming for us. He's coming for the wall."* |
| 4 | **Stone** | Bramble names it. **"Look at their feet."** They leave no prints |
| 5 | Enemy archers | They are wearing your colours. Under the rust, they are *yours* |
| 6 | Second expansion | **TWIST 1.** A soldier recognises a face. It is his father's |
| 7 | Marauders · shieldbearers | Wren starts dreaming. She does not mention it |
| 8 | **Iron** | Ilka surrenders instead of fighting, and will not say why until dawn |

| 10 | — | Bramble admits what she has known for forty years |
| 11 | Warlords | The oldest of them wear armour four hundred years out of fashion |

| 14–15 | Max Keep | The Rust stops sending scouts. He knows you are coming. He has been waiting |

## The betrayal — Lv. 12

Ser Bracken joins on night three with a horse and no explanation, is first over the wall, and carries
you through the hardest nights of the middle game. The player will like him enormously.

They have his daughter in the north, alive, and keep her that way monthly. At Keep 12 he opens the
east gate during a blood moon and stands aside. You lose the night, or very nearly.

He does not run. He is sitting on the step by the Keep door when you come to find him, sword laid
down beside him.

> **BRACKEN:** She was six. She's still six. They send me a lock of her hair at midwinter and the hair
> is still six years old. That's the deal, majesty. That's the whole deal. I'd do it again tonight.

**Then the player decides.** Hang him, exile him, or put him back on the wall. Each is a real branch
and each costs about a flag:

- **Hang him** — a permanent morale hit and two recruit mats lock for three nights. His daughter is
  never mentioned again.
- **Exile him** — he turns up at the worst possible moment later, on the other side, and it is worse.
- **Keep him** — he fights for you to the end and dies on the wall of the last night doing something
  genuinely stupid and brave.

The game's first real moral choice, and it costs almost nothing to build.

---

## And the raids do not stop

You kill the Rust, dawn comes, the kingdom celebrates — and the next night they come again. Fewer,
leaderless, but they come. He was one instrument. Something else is doing this and it has never once
been in the north.

Wren walks out to the gate at three in the morning, in her nightdress, facing north-west, and says it
out loud for the first time.

## What this asks you to build

| # | Thing | Effort | Buys |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The opening: the village stands, then falls, and they walk past you | Medium | The premise, the tutorial and the twist, in one scene |
| 2 | A `say()` bark system and a beat table keyed to `Lv.` | Small | The entire script |
| 3 | Reframe ranks, sappers and archers as the dead | Copy only | Twenty nights re-read at once |
| 4 | **Rooks carry bodies, not coins** | Small | The best mechanic in this document |
| 5 | Taken soldiers return with your own upgrades | Small | Makes losing hurt properly |
| 6 | The Rust drops his guard at 20%, the helm comes off | Small | World one's ending |
| 7 | Bracken's betrayal, and the hang / exile / keep choice | Small | The first real moral choice |

Items 2–6 are roughly a weekend and carry the whole game.

## What draft three had that this does not, and why

Kept out deliberately, and recoverable if the game ever grows into it:

- **The nine sisters, Ashaine, Cairnhold and the eight-hundred-year burial.** Replaced by one
  sentence. None of the mechanics needed the rest, and the player meets none of it in world one.
- **Maeve the Grey**, the two-hundred-year-old gaoler. Her only job in world one was to be an offscreen
  arm, and an offscreen arm does not need a biography.
- **Ilka's full arc** — the raider captain who surrenders. She survives as two lines because the payoff
  is real (raiders you have shot for thirty nights standing on your walls) but she is not load-bearing.
- **Worlds two and three.** Draft three planned a trilogy before world one worked. If world one lands,
  the one-sentence cosmology above expands in every direction it needs to.

The test this draft has to keep passing: **can the person who owns the game retell it from memory?**
If a beat cannot survive that, it is decoration and it goes.
