# What players say about the games next door

Ten games on the App Store are near enough to Crown Rush that their reviews are about *our* problems:
a hero you drive with a thumb, a village you pay for in coins, a wave that arrives on a clock, and a
390-pixel-wide screen to fit all of it on. This file is what their players say, sorted by the decision
it bears on.

**It is written, not measured**, and that distinction is the one `CLAUDE.md` draws. Nothing here is
regenerated by a tool, because there is nothing to count — it is a reading of a few hundred reviews,
and a reading is an opinion about what matters in them. Treat it the way you would treat
`docs/cast-review.md`: useful until somebody does the reading again, and honest about when that was.

**Provenance, because it is weaker than it looks.** The quotes were gathered on **20 September 2026**
through web search rather than by opening the stores — this environment's egress policy blocks
`apps.apple.com`, Metacritic, Reddit and Steam outright — so each one is a search engine's rendering
of a review rather than the review itself. They are quoted here as *evidence of a theme*, not as
testimony. Where a quote would change a decision on its own, go and read the original first; the last
section says where each one lives. The themes are safe: every one of them turned up independently in
three or more games.

## What they love, which is the part worth protecting

- **A short cycle that keeps moving.** The most-praised single mechanic in the Kingdom reviews is its
  day and night: *"the short day and night cycles keep the game moving at a pace that makes it hard to
  put down."* Our night is the same instrument.
- **Scarcity making a decision matter.** Bad North: *"you'll constantly be developing new strategies
  and trying to make the most of your limited number of troops."* Nobody says this about a game where
  the answer is to build more of everything.
- **Finding out by watching.** Kingdom's defenders like that you learn what a building does by
  building it. Even its critics concede that pleasure before complaining about the rest of it.
- **Art buys forgiveness.** In every one of these games the art style is the first thing praised, and
  it visibly softens the complaints that follow.

## What they complain about

### 1. "No tutorial" is the loudest theme, and it cuts both ways

Kingdom Two Crowns is the pure case: *"there is almost no text anywhere in the game. You don't know
what something will do until you build it and observe it in action."* Half its reviewers call that the
best thing about it. The other half report **looking it up on the internet**, and one draws the line
exactly: *"there is a fine line between explaining stuff and explaining almost nothing."* First
playthroughs get called overwhelming.

**For us:** the pad's `desc` behind a tap (#1253) is the middle path Kingdom refuses to take, and the
review evidence says it is right. Nobody in any of these games complains about too much text. They
complain about not knowing what a thing does before paying for it.

### 2. Slow reads as *relaxing* until it reads as *a waiting game*

The same game, from two reviewers: *"a pace that makes it hard to put down"*, and winter as *"an awful
slog [that] turns into a waiting game running to every bush to activate with coin."* Another:
*"a long slow crawl to beat everything."*

The line between the two is whether the slow part contains a **decision**. It is not about seconds.

**For us:** the breather is currently mostly walking. That is the shape of the thing they call a slog,
and it is the most actionable finding in this file.

### 3. Repetition kills this genre faster than difficulty does

Bad North holds a 7.8 user score with **40% of reviews rated mixed**, and the mixed ones converge:
*"the game becomes very repetitive very fast"*, *"each level plays out in virtually the same way"*,
*"too shallow"*, *"not enough meat on the bone."* This is a game whose art and feel are almost
universally admired. Repetition beat the admiration anyway.

**For us:** fifteen nights and thirty nights draw on the same pad tree, and a length changes only when
the camp opens and what a Keep level costs. That is the variable those reviewers are sensitive to, and
the long run is where it is most exposed.

### 4. The difficulty complaint is about the *step*, not the height

Bad North: *"the difficulty curve suddenly shoots upward at the final third."* Archero 2: *"if you're
not one-shotting enemies there isn't enough space to avoid damage, resulting in guaranteed hits."*

Nobody writes a review complaining a game was hard. They complain about the night where what had been
working stopped working with nothing said beforehand.

### 5. Touch is where mobile versions die, and the reports are specific

| Game | What players say |
| --- | --- |
| Vampire Survivors | *"the virtual joystick speed is painfully sluggish"*; no sensitivity setting; no haptics; *"unreliable controls easily become the main enemy"* |
| Kingdom Two Crowns | *"the game doesn't register tapping on objects to upgrade them or to pay coins"* — in a game designed gesture-first with no on-screen buttons |
| ISLANDERS: Mobile | *"selection and rotation of buildings are clunky"* |
| Brotato | players keep catching the **iOS home indicator** at the bottom of the screen mid-dodge |

**For us:** three systems sit exactly here — the drag-anywhere joystick, the build ghost's
drag-against-tap discrimination (`longSlop` is solving a problem with a body count), and anything
placed in the bottom strip of the screen. The Kingdom finding is the sharpest: a game can be designed
touch-first by a good studio and still have a tap that does not land, because *nearly* landing is
invisible to everyone but the player.

### 6. Scaling the UI up is not a fix

Northgard's port is the cautionary tale: *"the UI can be resized to several different increments for
readability, but then the UI feels cluttered on a small screen, which gets in the way of touch-based
map controls"*, and *"quick-select icons intended to help don't read very well and rarely felt
useful."* Vampire Survivors: *"the cluttered screen can make it difficult to track your character."*
Bloons, on a six-inch phone: *"unit deployment fiddly."*

**For us:** this is the best outside argument for the parked minimap (#145) that exists. Northgard's
players specifically resent a map-adjacent element that owns screen and obstructs the thing under it —
which is the charge #145 brought against ours. It also says what bringing it back would have to prove:
not that it is useful, but that it is more useful than the pixels it takes.

### 7. Performance arrives in the store as a rating, not as a backlog item

Northgard: *"very laggy after more than 15 minutes of play, with frame rates dropping and never
returning, especially if you get a phone call or text."* Vampire Survivors: lag scaling with the crowd.
ISLANDERS: *"slow from the start even with no buildings placed"* — noticed because the original is
*"buttery smooth."*

Two details in the Northgard one are worth sitting with: **fifteen minutes**, and **never returning**.
That is thermal throttling plus a lost context, and our runs are nineteen and thirty-seven minutes —
both past it. The draw-call and triangle budgets in the README are defending against the thing people
write one-star reviews about. (The download-size budget is parked and stays parked; this is not an
argument about bytes.)

### 8. A run has to survive a phone

The roguelite complaint, in players' words: *"players with limited playtime report being unable to
finish runs and view the lack of quick save as making the game unplayable"*, and *"losing progress due
to the game crashing and not saving has a negative impact on player retention."* A two-hour Bad North
run lost to a first defeat gets named specifically.

**For us:** nineteen minutes is comfortably inside what a phone player tolerates. Thirty-seven is at
the edge where this complaint starts, which makes the pause-on-tab-switch guard (#946) load-bearing
rather than polish, and makes the long run the thing to watch if retention ever gets measured.

### 9. Monetisation, as a list of what not to do

Brotato on mobile: *"heavily relies on ads, as they're literally required to progress."* Archero 2:
*"advertising the game as free only for players to hit a paywall after the first boss is
disingenuous"*, and twenty ads to unlock a deck. Being free with nothing to buy is a real
differentiator in this category, and these reviews are the argument for keeping it.

## Where to read the originals

Do the reading before betting anything on a single quote above.

- **App Store, "see all reviews"** — [Bad North](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/bad-north-jotunn-edition/id1367121168),
  [Kingdom Two Crowns](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/kingdom-two-crowns/id1477991646?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone),
  [Vampire Survivors](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/6444525702?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone),
  [Northgard](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/northgard/id1533979882?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone),
  [ISLANDERS: Mobile](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/islanders-mobile/id6745312000).
- **Negative reviews, pre-filtered.** `appsupports.co` serves one app's one-star and two-star reviews
  on a page of their own — [Bloons TD 6](https://appsupports.co/1118115766/bloons-td-6/negative-reviews),
  and swap the numeric App Store id for any other. This is the fastest hundred complaints available
  anywhere; the five-star page is where nothing is learned.
- **Metacritic user reviews**, which is where the mixed 40% live —
  [Bad North](https://www.metacritic.com/game/bad-north/user-reviews/),
  [Jotunn Edition](https://www.metacritic.com/game/bad-north-jotunn-edition/user-reviews/).
- **Pocket Gamer's App Army** — a panel of ordinary players writing a paragraph each, rather than one
  critic writing a verdict:
  [Kingdom Two Crowns](https://www.pocketgamer.com/kingdom-two-crowns/app-army-assemble-kingdom-two-crowns-is-this-acclaimed-side-scrolling-strategy-g/).
- **Steam discussions** for the desktop versions — longer, and about design rather than ports:
  [Kingdom Two Crowns](https://steamcommunity.com/app/701160/discussions/0/3200365942089565125/).

## Refreshing this

It goes stale the way the old brand page went stale, and for the same reason: nothing here is wired to
anything. The useful habit is to redo it after playing rather than instead of playing — a theme you
have felt yourself is worth ten you have read. When it is redone, **replace the themes rather than
appending to them**, and move the date at the top. A list that has grown by accretion is a list nobody
can tell the current opinion from.
